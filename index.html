Real Voices of History

A curated research guide for the cross-curricular monologue and dialogue performance assignment — Canadian History 1713–1800.

History: Historical Perspective Language: Oral Communication Drama: Role & Character 14 Historical Figures 7 Suggested Pairings

You have followed six fictional characters through three turning points in Canadian history. Now it is time to go deeper. Below are 14 real people who lived through the same events. Choose one for a monologue, or choose two for a dialogue. Your job is to research your figure(s) carefully and bring their real voice — their fears, beliefs, contradictions, and humanity — to life.

The 14 Historical Figures

Organized by perspective. Each card includes a research starting point and a suggested monologue moment.

Acadian Perspective

— Deportation, displacement, survival
Beausoleil Broussard
c. 1702 – 1786 · Acadian guerrilla leader, Nova Scotia
Led armed Acadian resistance against the British deportation. Captured twice, escaped twice. Eventually helped establish the Cajun community in Louisiana. One of the most defiant figures of the deportation era — a man who refused to accept expulsion as final.
Research Hook Why did Broussard keep fighting when almost everyone else had surrendered? What did resistance mean to someone who had lost everything?
💬 Monologue Moment: Speaking to his people after capture, urging them not to give up
Pélagie LeBlanc
Historical (fictionalized in Antonine Maillet's novel Pélagie-la-Charrette, 1979)
While a literary figure rather than a documented individual, Pélagie represents the thousands of Acadian women who led families back to the Maritimes after the deportation. Maillet's novel — based on oral history — won the Prix Goncourt. Pélagie is considered a cultural icon of Acadian survival and is grounded in real collective memory.
Research Hook What was the actual Acadian return like? How did families navigate being refugees for decades? Research the real return journeys of Acadian families 1763–1790.
💬 Monologue Moment: Returning to find her family's farmland occupied by strangers

Mi'kmaq & Indigenous Perspectives

— Sovereignty, treaty rights, displacement
Chief Jean-Baptiste Cope
c. 1690s – c. 1760 · Mi'kmaq Chief, Nova Scotia
Signed the Treaty of 1752 with the British on behalf of the Shubenacadie Mi'kmaq — a Peace and Friendship Treaty that did not surrender land but recognized Mi'kmaq rights. When the British violated the treaty almost immediately, Cope felt the weight of having trusted them. A figure who tried diplomacy and watched it fail.
Research Hook What did the 1752 treaty actually say? Why did Cope sign it? What happened afterward that made the treaty meaningless in practice?
💬 Monologue Moment: Addressing British officials after the treaty has been broken
Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea)
1743 – 1807 · Mohawk Chief & British ally, Upper Canada
One of the most complex figures of this era. A Mohawk leader who was educated in English, converted to Christianity, fought for the British during the American Revolution, translated the Bible into Mohawk, and then spent years fighting — mostly unsuccessfully — for Haudenosaunee land rights after the Loyalist migration pushed into his people's territory.
Research Hook Brant is sometimes called a "collaborator" and sometimes a defender of his people. Was he both? Research the Grand River land negotiations and what Brant was actually trying to achieve.
💬 Monologue Moment: Addressing British officials demanding the land grants he was promised

French-Canadian Perspective

— Conquest, survival of language and culture
Marie-Josephte Corriveau
1733 – 1763 · French-Canadian, Québec
Convicted of murdering her second husband and executed by British military court just after the conquest of New France. Her body was displayed in a gibbet (iron cage) in public — a practice unheard of in New France. She became a folk legend, a symbol of French-Canadian resistance to British authority. Her trial reveals how different British and French legal systems were.
Research Hook Was Corriveau's trial fair? What does her story tell us about life for French Canadians under new British rule? Why did she become a legend?
💬 Monologue Moment: Speaking before the British court that will sentence her
Bishop Jean-Olivier Briand
1715 – 1794 · Catholic Bishop of Québec
Led the Catholic Church in Québec through the British conquest. Navigated an almost impossible position: maintaining the Church's authority under a Protestant British government, while keeping the loyalty of French-Canadian parishioners. He argued for accommodation with Britain rather than resistance — and was deeply controversial for doing so.
Research Hook Should Briand have resisted British rule more openly, or was his strategy of accommodation the only realistic way to protect French-Canadian culture? Research the Quebec Act of 1774 and Briand's role in it.
💬 Monologue Moment: Explaining to his congregation why they must accept British rule

British Colonial Perspective

— Empire, governance, moral complexity
Guy Carleton (Lord Dorchester)
1724 – 1808 · Governor of Québec & Upper Canada
British governor who championed the Quebec Act (1774), arguing that French Canadians should keep their language, religion, and laws under British rule. This was controversial in Britain and infuriated American colonists. Later governed during the Loyalist migration, where he also advocated for Black Loyalists' land rights — imperfectly, but more than most.
Research Hook Why did Carleton, a British general, fight to protect French-Canadian culture? Was he genuinely principled, or was it political strategy? Research his arguments for the Quebec Act.
💬 Monologue Moment: Defending the Quebec Act to critics in the British Parliament
John Graves Simcoe
1752 – 1806 · First Lieutenant-Governor of Upper Canada
Founded York (now Toronto), built Yonge Street, and — most significantly — passed the Act Against Slavery in Upper Canada in 1793. This was among the first anti-slavery legislation in the British Empire. He was a Loyalist military hero who turned out to have a genuine moral conviction on one of the era's darkest questions.
Research Hook Simcoe's anti-slavery act was limited — it didn't free people already enslaved. Was it a real moral stand or a political compromise? Research what the act actually did and didn't do.
💬 Monologue Moment: Announcing the anti-slavery act to the Upper Canada legislature

Black Loyalist & Enslaved Perspectives

— Freedom, broken promises, resilience
David George
c. 1742 – 1810 · Black Loyalist minister, Nova Scotia & Sierra Leone
Born into slavery in Virginia. Escaped, became a Baptist preacher, arrived in Birchtown, Nova Scotia in 1783. Survived the Shelburne Riots when white Loyalists attacked the Black community. Eventually led a group of Black Loyalists to Sierra Leone in 1792, concluding that true equality was not available in Canada. A preacher who kept faith in God while losing faith in the British promise.
Research Hook David George wrote a memoir. Why did he eventually leave Canada? What did Birchtown actually look like — research the living conditions and compare them to what Black Loyalists were promised.
💬 Monologue Moment: Preaching to his congregation in Birchtown after the Shelburne Riots
Black Loyalist Women of Birchtown
1783 onward · Documented in the Book of Negroes
The Book of Negroes (1783) is a real document — a British registry of over 3,000 Black Loyalists who boarded ships to Nova Scotia. It records names, ages, former enslavers, and brief physical descriptions. Women like Violet King, Mary Postell, and Nancy Freedom are listed by name. Their individual stories can be partially reconstructed from this document and historical records.
Research Hook Search the digitized Book of Negroes (Library and Archives Canada). Find a real woman's entry. What can you infer about her life from just a few words of description? Research what daily life in Birchtown was like for women.
💬 Monologue Moment: A woman describing her first winter in Birchtown to a friend
Chloe Cooley
fl. 1793 · Enslaved woman, Upper Canada
Chloe Cooley's name appears in history because of one act of resistance. When her enslaver forcibly transported her across the Niagara River to sell her to an American buyer, she screamed and fought back so visibly that witnesses reported it to Lieutenant-Governor Simcoe. Her resistance directly triggered Simcoe's Act Against Slavery (1793). She disappeared from the historical record after that — but her scream changed Canadian law.
Research Hook We know almost nothing about Chloe Cooley's life before or after this moment. Research what is documented. What does it mean that her one recorded act was an act of resistance? Why did Simcoe act?
💬 Monologue Moment: The moment of resistance at the riverbank — what she was thinking

Loyalist Settler Perspective

— Displacement, loyalty, building Upper Canada
Barbara Heck
1734 – 1804 · Loyalist settler & Methodist founder, Upper Canada
An Irish-born Loyalist who had previously helped establish Methodism in New York, then fled to Upper Canada after the Revolution. Known as the "Mother of Methodism in Canada." She rebuilt her religious community from scratch in the wilderness, organizing worship before there were any church buildings. A woman who carried her faith across two migrations.
Research Hook What drove Heck to keep rebuilding her religious community after losing everything twice? Research what Loyalist life looked like in early Upper Canada — what did settlers actually face in the wilderness?
💬 Monologue Moment: Leading the first Methodist meeting in her new Upper Canada settlement
Peter Martin (Ohtowankannin)
c. 1750 – 1847 · Mohawk Loyalist, Upper Canada
A Mohawk man who served as an interpreter and scout for the British during the American Revolution. He occupied a unique position: an Indigenous man loyal to the British Crown, who then faced the same displacement as other Loyalists but with the added dimension of being from a nation whose land the Loyalists were being settled on. His story sits at the intersection of every major tension of this era.
Research Hook How did Mohawk Loyalists experience the Loyalist migration differently from white Loyalists? Research the Six Nations land at the Grand River — what were they promised, and what did they actually receive?
💬 Monologue Moment: Watching Loyalist settlers arrive on Haudenosaunee land

Additional Voices

— Jewish-Canadian & lesser-known perspectives
Aaron Hart
1724 – 1800 · Jewish-Canadian merchant, Trois-Rivières
One of the first Jewish settlers in Canada, arriving in Trois-Rivières with the British army after the conquest. Became a successful merchant and fur trader. His family's story — navigating both French-Canadian and British colonial society as Jews — reveals a community rarely discussed in Canadian history. His son Ezekiel Hart was later elected to the Legislative Assembly but refused his seat because he would not swear a Christian oath.
Research Hook What was life like for Jewish people in post-conquest Québec? Research Aaron Hart's business dealings and his family's legal struggles for equal rights.
💬 Monologue Moment: Explaining his decision to settle in Trois-Rivières to a skeptical relative

Suggested Dialogue Pairings

Seven pairings — four historically grounded, three imaginative. Each has a central tension to explore.

How to read these: "Historical" means these two people actually met or interacted. "Imaginative" means they lived in the same era and world but have no documented meeting — you must infer what they would say to each other based on research.

Historical
Joseph Brant in dialogue with Guy Carleton
Brant negotiated directly with Carleton over Haudenosaunee land rights along the Grand River. Carleton made promises he could not fully keep. This dialogue sits at the heart of the tension between British good intentions and colonial reality.
What do you owe me, Governor? I fought for your King. I gave up everything. You gave me a river and a promise — and now the settlers are crossing the river.
Historical
Chloe Cooley in dialogue with John Graves Simcoe
Cooley's resistance was the direct trigger for Simcoe's anti-slavery act. They almost certainly never met — but their lives intersected with historic consequence. This pairing explores power, action, and who gets credit for change.
You passed a law because of what you saw done to me. But the law didn't free me. So what, exactly, did you do for me?
Historical
David George in dialogue with John Graves Simcoe
Simcoe's anti-slavery act came too late for most Black Loyalists already in Nova Scotia. George and the Birchtown community were living proof that legal freedom and actual equality were different things. This pairing asks: what does justice actually require?
You freed the ones not yet born into slavery. But what about the ones already living in Birchtown, cheated of our land, attacked in our homes — what did your law do for us?
Historical
Bishop Briand in dialogue with Marie-Josephte Corriveau
Briand advocated accommodation with British rule; Corriveau became a symbol of French resistance to it. Their worldviews are a direct collision: survival through cooperation versus resistance at any cost. (Note: they likely knew of each other given the small size of Québec society at the time.)
You tell us to accept British law, Bishop. And British law put me in a cage for all of Québec to see. Is that your accommodation?
Imaginative
Beausoleil Broussard in dialogue with David George
Two men — one Acadian, one Black Loyalist — who both experienced violent displacement by the British Empire, both refused to accept defeat, and both eventually left for somewhere they hoped would be better. What would they say to each other about resistance, faith, and whether the fight was worth it?
You fought the British with a musket. I fought them with a Bible and a congregation. Tell me — which of us did more damage?
Imaginative
Chief Jean-Baptiste Cope in dialogue with Beausoleil Broussard
A Mi'kmaq chief who tried diplomacy and a French-Acadian who chose armed resistance — both dealing with the same British expansion, both watching it destroy their world. This pairing explores two different philosophies of response to colonial power.
I signed a treaty. You took up arms. Neither of us stopped what happened. Tell me, Broussard — was there a right answer, or only two different ways of losing?
Imaginative
Barbara Heck in dialogue with a Black Loyalist woman of Birchtown
Two women who arrived in the same colonial moment, both displaced, both starting over — one with a land grant and community support, one with a smaller grant, worse land, and neighbours who would eventually riot against her. Both called themselves Loyalists. What did that word mean differently to each of them?
We came on the same ships. We are both Loyalists. We both built something from nothing in this wilderness. So why does everything I look at tell me we are not equal?

The Assignment

📖 Option A — Monologue

  • Choose one historical figure
  • Research their life, beliefs, and historical moment
  • Write a 2–3 minute monologue in first person
  • Perform it for the class in character
  • Include a 1-page research summary (not performed)

🤝 Option B — Dialogue

  • Choose two figures (from suggested pairings or propose your own)
  • Each partner researches one figure independently
  • Write a 3–5 minute dialogue script together
  • Perform it for the class in character
  • Each partner submits their own 1-page research summary

📚 Research Requirements

  • At least 2 reliable sources (not Wikipedia as primary)
  • Must reference at least one real documented event or quote
  • Character's beliefs must be historically defensible
  • Research summary must cite your sources

🎭 Performance Requirements

  • Stay in character for the full performance
  • Use period-appropriate language (no slang)
  • Show the character's specific emotional truth
  • Speak to a real historical moment — not generic history

Assessment Rubric

Cross-curricular: History (Historical Perspective) · Language (Oral Communication) · Drama (Role & Character) — each weighted equally.

Strand / Criteria 4 — Excellent 3 — Proficient 2 — Developing 1 — Beginning
📜 Historical Accuracy
History		 Specific, documented events and beliefs accurately represented. Research is evident and woven naturally into the performance. Most key facts are accurate. One or two details may be imprecise but do not misrepresent the character's historical position. General accuracy, but character's historical moment is vague or generic. Little evidence of specific research. Significant historical errors or character's situation is not grounded in the period.
🌍 Historical Perspective
History		 Character's worldview is fully inhabited — their fears, contradictions, and beliefs are specific to their time and position. Avoids projecting modern values. Character shows an understanding of their historical perspective with some depth. Minor modern anachronisms present. Character's perspective is present but surface-level. Some modern values projected onto historical figure. Little attempt to inhabit the character's actual historical worldview.
🗣️ Oral Communication
Language		 Speaks clearly and with purpose. Language choices feel intentional and period-appropriate. Engages the audience with pace and emphasis. Communicates clearly. Some variation in pace and emphasis. Language is generally appropriate to the period. Communication is understandable but flat. Limited variation in delivery. Some anachronistic language. Difficult to follow. Reads directly from script with little engagement or vocal presence.
✍️ Writing Quality
Language		 Script/monologue is well-structured, uses vivid and precise language, and builds to a clear emotional or argumentative point. Script is structured and clear. Language is appropriate. The character's main point comes through. Script is understandable but loosely structured. Language is basic. The character's point is unclear. Script lacks structure. Ideas are disconnected. Difficult to follow the character's perspective.
🎭 Role & Character
Drama		 Fully commits to character. Physicality, voice, and emotional truth are consistent throughout. The audience believes the character, not the student. Character commitment is mostly sustained. Occasional breaks in role, but the overall performance is believable. Some commitment to character but frequently breaks role or seems unsure of the character's emotional truth. Little to no character commitment. Performs as themselves rather than as the historical figure.
💬 Dialogue (if applicable)
Drama		 Characters genuinely listen and respond to each other. The tension of the historical relationship is palpable. The dialogue feels like a real encounter, not two separate monologues. Partners respond to each other meaningfully. Some moments of real tension. Mostly avoids talking "past" each other. Partners take turns speaking but do not always genuinely respond to what the other says. Feels like adjacent monologues. No real interaction between characters. Dialogue is two disconnected speeches.
